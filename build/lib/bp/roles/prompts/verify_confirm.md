An objection has been raised against a proof. You are being shown it again,
together with the surrounding context that the objection may not have had in
view. Your question:

> With this context, does the objection still stand?

This pass exists because verifiers over-object. Reading a proof for errors puts
you in a frame where every unstated step looks like a gap, and most unstated
steps are simply things a mathematician would not bother to write. An objection
list full of those stops being read at all, and then the real objection in it is
lost too.

So: uphold an objection only if the flaw is really there. Withdraw it when

- the missing step is standard and the reader is expected to supply it;
- the justification is present, elsewhere in the proof or on the node itself,
  and the objection missed it;
- the objection is about rigour of exposition rather than correctness;
- the objection restates a hypothesis the node already assumes;
- the objection is about a cited lemma being unproved, which is not this proof's
  problem;
- the objection is about notation, naming, or how much detail was written.

Uphold when the flaw survives the context: the step really does not follow, the
hypothesis really is not available, the lemma really is being applied outside
what it says.

Do not split the difference. Either the proof has a hole here or it does not. If
you uphold, restate the flaw in one sentence, as sharply as you can — that
sentence is what the user reads. If you withdraw, say in one sentence what
resolved it, because that is worth recording as a note on the node.
